Background

Biology learns to write

For most of human history the natural world looked like a fixed backdrop. That model no longer holds. Human activity is now the dominant driver of change in Earth’s natural systems, enough that scientists have proposed we live in a new geological epoch, the Anthropocene (Crutzen 2006). We are terraforming our own planet, mostly by accident. There is a hard shard of hope in that. A species that can warm a planet with no shared intent has proven something about its reach; the same collective power, aimed on purpose, might move things back.

Biology is one of the tools for aiming it. A genetic sequence can be treated as a function written in the programming language of life, standardized and snapped together with others into genetic circuits (Way et al. 2014). Synthetic biology is descriptive biology picking up the pen. Cells can be engineered to make fuels without a drill, or to pull carbon back out of the air. The same command over living systems can also be turned toward harm, and the dual-use worry is real and widely discussed (DeLisi et al. 2020). This is a technology with enormous potential to help, genuine potential to harm, and a trajectory that is still being set.

Two falling cost curves drive the field. DNA sequencing has dropped faster than computing’s Moore’s Law: the human genome cost about three billion dollars to sequence in 2003 and a few hundred today. DNA synthesis, writing a sequence from scratch, has followed. As both get cheaper, more people can design and test biological systems, and what Drew Endy called the “foundations” of a new engineering discipline in 2005 has been built out into an industry (Endy 2005).

A field that is hard to see

Policymakers cannot fund what they cannot find, and synthetic biology is unusually hard to see from the outside. It grew out of molecular biology and shares much of its vocabulary. A researcher studying the metabolic pathways of E. coli with no intention of engineering them uses the same words as a synthetic biologist who does. Oldham and colleagues hit exactly this wall building systematic maps of the field: any keyword query drags in a large mass of descriptive molecular biology (Oldham and Hall 2018).

Keyword drift makes it worse. The field names its methods as it discovers them, so today’s central technique was yesterday’s obscure acronym. CRISPR was a little-known term long before it meant gene editing; a query written in 2010 would miss the root of nearly all the modern genome-editing literature. Drawing a clean, stable boundary around synthetic biology is a genuine problem, and it shaped many choices in this project.

This matters because young fields are where good early decisions have the most leverage. Government acts not only as a funder but as an early customer and partner that helps a technology cross from lab to market (Doblinger et al. 2019). The catch is the shape of the problem for policy: the fields with the most to gain from early steering are the ones we can see least clearly. A synthetic biology that is easy to measure will already be a mature one, with its trajectory mostly set.

iGEM and the BioBrick registry

Synthetic biology comes with a data source most young fields lack. The International Genetically Engineered Machine competition, iGEM, is a worldwide student contest somewhere between a science fair and a startup accelerator. Teams, usually hosted by a university, spend most of a year building an original project from a standardized kit of DNA parts, and they document it in an open format on public wikis (Santolini et al. 2023). For many students this is the on-ramp into the field. By collecting thousands of these projects, we get a detailed, open record of a field composing itself.

The kit is the BioBrick registry. A BioBrick is a genetic part in a common physical format: flanking restriction sites let any BioBrick-compatible part slot into any BioBrick-compatible vector (Shetty et al. 2008). It is LEGO logic for genetics. A promoter deposited by a team in Uppsala is, in principle, combinable with a part deposited by a team in Auckland. The registry has grown to roughly 89,000 parts, each tagged with a stable BBa_ identifier, a functional class, and often a link back to the paper the sequence came from. That standardization is what turns a pile of one-off sequences into a genuine shared toolkit, and it is what lets us treat the parts as a second, independent record of what students actually built.

Booze Bugs: one thread, start to finish

The whole project fits inside one story. In 2009 the Uppsala iGEM team built Booze Bugs: Sun to Alcohol, engineering Synechocystis cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) to turn sunlight and carbon dioxide into ethanol and butanol. Two team members later carried that line of work into a research lab, and a 2014 publication on cyanobacterial promoters cites the team’s own BioBrick, a copper-inducible promoter, by its registry code (Camsund et al. 2014). A competition project became peer-reviewed science, in the same city, traceable through a single physical part. That thread, from student bench to publication, is the thesis in miniature, and iGEM produces threads like it all over the world.

Cyanobacteria earn their place here twice. They are among the oldest photosynthesizers on Earth, and they are a favorite chassis for engineered carbon capture: cells that eat sunlight and CO₂ and can be tuned to make fuel. They are also a topic that unites students, academic labs, and patent-filing firms in the same cities. Carbon capture is the case study threaded through this whole project, the place where we watch the general method land on something concrete. It is also the proof that the pen can heal as well as harm.

The geography of innovation

Research is not spread evenly across the map, and it does not move at random. A place tends to branch into activities related to what it already does, because the skills and knowledge carry over (Neffke and Henning 2013). This is the principle of relatedness: as a city builds capability in one technology, it grows its advantage in nearby ones (Hidalgo et al. 2018; Hidalgo et al. 2007). A city scaling industrial fermentation of ethanol is better positioned to move into other biofuels than a city with no such base.

Why would student and academic work align locally at all? A few mechanisms could do it. The same faculty advise students and run labs, so their shared interests pull both toward the same topics. A city’s firms, funders, and institutions form a common information environment that shapes what everyone works on. And students sort into universities partly by research strength. These are not mutually exclusive, and this data cannot cleanly separate them. Boschma’s proximity framework is the useful frame here: sharing a neighborhood creates the potential for knowledge exchange, but sharing a knowledge base is what makes the exchange productive (Boschma 2005). Cognitive proximity, not just geographic proximity, is what we are after.

Measuring relatedness in a young field

Economic geography usually measures relatedness with labor flows, product similarity, or co-patent networks. Those tools were built for large, mature industries and need data, worker mobility, product co-export, that simply do not exist for a student-to-academia-to-industry pipeline in an emerging field. Local knowledge spillovers are known to be geographically sticky (Breschi and Lissoni 2001), but measuring them across three such different artifact types calls for a different instrument.

The instrument is semantic distance. A language model reads the title and abstract of a document and turns it into a vector, a list of numbers that encodes meaning. Represent those vectors as points in a high-dimensional space, and documents about similar things land near one another, whatever words they use. We use SPECTER2, a model trained to predict which papers cite one another, so semantic closeness stands in for the kind of relatedness a citation would mark (Singh et al. 2023). That is the core beneath every comparison here, and the reason we can put a student wiki, a journal abstract, and a patent claim on the same map.

There is an honest caveat. SPECTER2 was trained on academic papers and had never seen a patent or a student wiki. Precedent from patent-domain embedding work suggests the model transfers, and we fine-tune it on links between our own artifact types to close the gap. The Methods page describes how, and the Results show that the central finding survives even on the base model, before any fine-tuning.
