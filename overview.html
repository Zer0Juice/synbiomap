Overview

Do a city’s students, academics, and inventors in synthetic biology work on the same things?

For most of its history, biology read nature. It broke life into organs, then cells, then the genes beneath them, and slowly worked out the rules that hold a living thing together. Synthetic biology is the moment biology starts to write. We now compose new biological systems from standardized genetic parts, treating DNA as code and cells as hardware. The same power that has been reshaping the planet by accident could, aimed on purpose, help repair it. Engineered microbes can already pull carbon dioxide out of the air and turn it into fuel.

The students in iGEM are learning to hold that pen. Every year, thousands of them design and build a project from a shared kit of interchangeable DNA parts, and they write it all up on a public wiki. That makes iGEM something most young fields never get: an open, geotagged record of a field composing itself. This project asks what that record can tell us about where synthetic biology is putting down roots.

The question

Do cities whose students research a topic in synthetic biology also produce academic papers, and patents, in related areas? We put student projects, academic papers, and patents into one shared semantic space and test whether a city’s three kinds of work land on the same specific topics more often than chance allows.

The short answer is a qualified yes, with a specific shape. A city’s papers line up with its projects, and its papers line up with its patents, but its projects and its patents stay further apart. Academic research is the middle link. The field is shaped like a star, with papers at the hub, not a triangle.

The corpus in numbers

Artifact type Count Years
Academic papers (OpenAlex) 10,319 2000–2026
iGEM student projects 4,606 2009–2025
USPTO patents (Oldham set) 2,901 —
Total artifacts 17,826

All three are geocoded to a city and embedded into the same 768-dimensional space with a fine-tuned SPECTER2 model, then grouped into 80 topic clusters. 156 cities carry all three artifact types at once.

NoteA note on language

This work is descriptive. We measure relatedness and co-location, and we test them hard, but we do not claim that student projects cause later papers or that papers cause patents. The three share many local causes, and observational data like this cannot separate them.
